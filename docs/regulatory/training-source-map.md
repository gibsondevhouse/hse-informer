# Regulatory training source map

> Supplied 2026-10-02. Scope: Federal OSHA general industry. Controlled planning reference, not legal advice. Site applicability and current regulatory text require review before release.

## Supplied source map

For a chemical-manufacturing LMS, “compliant training” is not just assigning a course and recording a quiz score. OSHA generally expects training to be **job- and site-specific, understandable, delivered before exposure or assignment, reinforced when conditions change, and supported by documentation and demonstrated competency where the standard requires it. The source of truth should be the applicable regulation—not a generic course library. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA2254.pdf)

Below is an LMS-ready regulatory source map and the minimum training elements for each program. I assume **Federal OSHA general industry** applies in Georgia, and that your facilities include manufacturing, warehousing, maintenance, and potentially QC/R&D labs. Confirm scope for each site: laboratory operations, emergency responders, respirators, energized electrical work, forklift use, and personnel with reasonably anticipated exposure to blood/OPIM.

## Source Documents

Use these as the controlled regulatory references in your LMS curriculum library. Link each course to the standard paragraph(s), your site-specific written program/procedure, relevant SDSs, equipment manuals, and task JHAs.

| Training subject | Primary regulatory source | Core training trigger | Written program / documentation relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory protection | 29 CFR 1910.134, especially (c), (e), (f), (k), (l), (m) | Required respirator use, and voluntary use other than dust-mask-only use | Written respiratory protection program; medical evaluations, fit testing, training, inspections, and program evaluation records |
| Electrical safety | 29 CFR 1910.331–.335; especially 1910.332 | Employees exposed to electrical shock hazards not reduced to a safe level by installation requirements | Electrical-safe-work program/procedures; qualification records; energized-work authorization where applicable |
| Heat/thermal stress | OSH Act §5(a)(1), OSHA heat guidance/inspection guidance; applicable 1910 standards such as sanitation, PPE, medical services, and outdoor/environment-specific hazards | Recognized indoor or outdoor heat exposure capable of causing illness or serious harm | Written heat illness prevention plan is strongly advisable; document hazard assessment, acclimatization, WBGT/heat-index monitoring, training, emergency response |
| Lockout/tagout | 29 CFR 1910.147, especially (c)(1), (c)(4), (c)(6), (c)(7) | Servicing/maintenance where unexpected energization, startup, or stored-energy release could injure employees | Energy-control program; machine-specific procedures unless a documented exception applies; annual periodic-inspection certifications; training certifications |

| Walking-working surfaces | 29 CFR 1910 Subpart D, especially 1910.22 and 1910.30 | Employees exposed to covered fall hazards or using covered equipment; task-specific coverage for dockboards, ladders, rope descent systems, etc. | Inspection/repair system; fall-protection procedures; qualification and retraining records |

For OSHA’s own program templates and supplemental materials, its sample-program page includes model HazCom and bloodborne pathogen plans, a minimal LOTO procedure, and respiratory-program resources. Those materials are useful references, but they do **not** replace facility-specific programs and procedures. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/complianceassistance/sampleprograms)

## What “Compliant” Means

Your LMS needs to support the following baseline requirements across all subjects:

- **Training must reach the right population.** Assign by job role, work area, task authorization, chemical/process exposure, and equipment type—not just department.
- **Training must be understandable.** OSHA specifically requires understandable training for walking-working-surface/fall-protection training, and this is a sound audit standard across the program. Use the worker’s language and literacy level; a completion click or English-only slide deck is not proof of comprehension. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.30)
- **Training must occur before the worker performs the hazardous task or enters the exposed assignment.** This is explicit for HazCom, chemical hygiene, bloodborne pathogen exposure, PIT operation, and respirator use. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)
- **Training must be site specific.** Your LMS content needs to incorporate actual plant procedures, chemical inventory/SDS access, emergency contacts, eyewash/shower locations, alarm routes, energy-isolation points, traffic plans, rack conditions, and assigned PPE.
- **Some subjects require performance verification—not just an exam.** Forklift operation requires practical training and workplace performance evaluation; respirator users require medical clearance and fit testing in addition to training; LOTO requires annual procedure inspections; electrical “qualified person” status must be tied to demonstrated skills and assigned work. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)
- **Retraining must be event driven.** A universal annual refresher is not a substitute for retraining after a change in equipment, procedure, work area, chemical hazard, task, incident, near miss, failed evaluation, or observed unsafe practice.
- **Document proof, not merely attendance.** The defensible record includes trainee identity, date, instructor/qualified evaluator, subject and procedure/equipment version, assessment method and score, practical evaluation result where required, and retraining triggers.

## Subject Requirements

### Respiratory protection

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.134. If respirator use is required by exposure conditions or the employer, establish and implement a written respiratory-protection program with worksite-specific procedures, administered by a qualified program administrator. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910)

**Who must train**
- Every employee required to use a respirator.
- Voluntary users of elastomeric respirators or other respirators beyond voluntary dust-mask use require the applicable Appendix D information and program controls.
- Supervisors, respirator issuers, maintenance staff, emergency-response personnel, and fit-test personnel should receive role-specific training.

**Minimum LMS content**
- Why the respirator is necessary; the applicable hazard and likely exposure scenario.
- Respirator limitations and capabilities, including when a respirator is not protective.
- How improper fit, facial hair, eyewear, hoods, PPE interference, damaged components, or incorrect cartridges compromise protection.
- How to inspect, don, doff, user-seal-check, use, maintain, clean, store, and recognize malfunction.
- Cartridge/canister change schedule and end-of-service-life limitations.
- Emergency procedures and actions when the respirator fails.
- Medical signs or symptoms that may limit or prevent effective use.

**Frequency and competency**
- Initial training before required use.
- At least **annually**, and more often when workplace changes, respirator changes, knowledge gaps, or improper use indicate retraining is necessary. OSHA describes this training as comprehensive and understandable. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA2254.pdf)
- Require a hands-on demonstration: selection, inspection, donning, seal check, and cleaning/storage.
- Keep medical evaluation and fit-test status separate from course completion; training alone does not authorize use of a tight-fitting respirator.

**Inspection issue:** An OSHA compliance officer will compare employee training and fit-test records with the respirator models/cartridges actually used, SDS hazards, exposure assessments, facial-hair enforcement, and your written change schedule.

### Electrical safety

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.332, with safe-work-practice requirements in 1910.331–.335.

This standard applies to employees facing electrical shock risk not reduced to a safe level by compliant electrical installation. It does not mean every employee needs “qualified electrical worker” training. Qualification must be tied to actual duties involving exposed energized parts. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.332)

**Employee categories**
- **Qualified persons:** Workers permitted to work on or near exposed energized parts.
- **Unqualified persons with electrical exposure:** Maintenance-adjacent, operators, warehouse staff, contractors, or others who may encounter electrical hazards but are not permitted to conduct energized electrical work.
- **General awareness population:** Employees who may need hazard recognition, panel-clearance awareness, damaged-cord reporting, and no-access rules but do not fall within 1910.332’s scope.

**Minimum LMS content**
- Electrical hazard recognition: shock, arc flash, arc blast, burns, fire, stored electrical energy, wet locations, damaged cords, improper temporary power, and overloaded circuits.
- Site rules for panel clearance, access control, extension-cord/temporary-power use, reporting defects, and electrical-room entry.
- Work-practice controls under 1910.333: deenergization, verification, guarding, approach boundaries where applicable, and prohibition on unauthorized energized work.
- Electrical interface with LOTO, including the distinction between electrical safe-work-condition practices and equipment-energy-control procedures.
- For qualified persons: skills to distinguish exposed live parts, determine nominal voltage, and apply the required approach/clearance distances. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.332)

**Delivery and frequency**
- OSHA permits classroom or OJT delivery; the depth must match employee risk. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.332)
- The cited section does not impose a blanket annual retraining interval. Establish retraining based on job change, new equipment/technology, incidents/near misses, observed deficiencies, changes to safe-work practices, or loss of demonstrated proficiency.
- For actual energized electrical work, add NFPA 70E-based qualification and periodic retraining as a **consensus-standard/corporate best-practice layer**, clearly distinguished from OSHA’s regulatory minimum.

### Heat and thermal stress

**Federal OSHA status:** Federal OSHA currently does not have a specific heat-stress standard. However, OSHA may enforce hazardous heat exposures under the General Duty Clause, Section 5(a)(1), where the heat hazard is recognized, likely to cause death or serious physical harm, and feasibly correctable. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2001-10-17-0)

**Do not label heat training “1910-required annual training” unless a specific applicable rule or company policy requires it.** Instead, identify it accurately as a heat-illness-prevention program element supporting General Duty Clause compliance and recognized industrial-hygiene practice.

**Minimum LMS content**
- Heat sources at your plant: outdoor loading, roof work, tank farms, boiler/utility spaces, hot-process areas, steam leaks, PPE burden, confined or poorly ventilated spaces, and heat generated by physical work.
- Heat illness progression: heat rash, heat cramps, heat syncope, heat exhaustion, heat stroke; emphasize that heat stroke is a medical emergency.
- Signs, symptoms, individual risk factors, medication/medical-condition considerations, hydration, acclimatization, buddy observation, work/rest practices, cooling/recovery areas, and when not to work alone.
- Site-specific escalation: first aid, supervisor notification, radio/phone use, emergency response, cooling actions, and 911/emergency medical services activation.
- Procedures for new hires and workers returning after absence. OSHA has identified acclimatization and immediate response to symptoms as core heat-program elements. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2010-05-18)

**Frequency**
- Before hot-weather/hot-process assignment, before seasonal heat exposure, and when conditions or controls change.
- Provide pre-shift heat briefings or toolbox talks during elevated-heat conditions as an **industry best practice**, not as a standalone substitute for initial program training.
- Train both employees and supervisors. OSHA’s heat inspection guidance specifies worker training on hazards, prevention, symptoms, first aid, and the employer’s heat program. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2021-09-01)

**Monitoring layer**
For a chemical plant, build a separate supervisor/industrial-hygiene module on WBGT or other justified heat-exposure assessment, metabolic workload, clothing/PPE adjustment factors, acclimatization status, and ACGIH/NIOSH-based work-rest planning. That is a technical exposure-control module, not a generic employee awareness course.

### Lockout/tagout

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.147.

The employer must establish a program consisting of energy-control procedures, employee training, and periodic inspections before servicing or maintenance where unexpected startup, energization, or release of stored energy could cause injury. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)

**Training populations and minimum content**

| Population | OSHA-required training focus |
|---|---|
| Authorized employee | Recognition of applicable hazardous energy sources; type and magnitude of energy; methods and means for energy isolation and control |
| Affected employee | Purpose and use of the energy-control procedure |
| Other employee | Procedure awareness and prohibition against attempting to restart/reenergize locked or tagged equipment |

**LMS and practical-validation requirements**
- Train authorized employees on your site’s LOTO policy plus machine-/equipment-specific energy-control procedures.
- Cover all potentially hazardous energy: electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical, thermal, gravity, spring tension, pressure, stored fluid/gas, rotating energy, and process flow.
- Include shutdown, isolation, lock/tag application, stored-energy dissipation/restraint, verification of isolation, servicing, shift/group lockout, contractor coordination, and restoration to service.
- Require scenario-based content for chemical processes: line breaking, pump/mixer service, valves with bypasses, blocked-in pressure, nitrogen/inert-gas systems, thermal energy, feed systems, bulk handling, and control-system interfaces.
- Do not treat “push stop, disconnect switch, or control-circuit shutdown” as LOTO unless it achieves compliant energy isolation.

**Frequency and records**
- Retrain authorized and affected employees when job assignments, machines/processes, or procedures change; also when annual inspections or other evidence identifies deviations or knowledge gaps. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)
- Conduct and certify a **periodic inspection at least annually** for each energy-control procedure, performed by an authorized employee other than the employee using the procedure being inspected. Certification must identify the equipment/procedure, inspection date, employees included, and inspector. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)
- Certify training with each employee’s name and dates of training. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147)

**Common failure:** A generic annual LOTO PowerPoint does not prove that an authorized employee can isolate a particular reactor, packaging line, agitator, conveyor, dust collector, or transfer pump. Pair the LMS module with documented field verification against the actual procedure.

### Forklift / powered industrial trucks

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.178(l).

Before an employee operates a powered industrial truck other than as a supervised trainee, the employer must ensure successful completion of the required training and evaluation. Training must combine formal instruction, practical training, and workplace performance evaluation. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)

**Required structure**
- **Formal instruction:** Classroom, instructor-led, e-learning, video, written materials, or equivalent.
- **Practical training:** Trainer demonstration and trainee practical exercise.
- **Workplace evaluation:** Evaluation of the operator’s performance in the actual workplace.
- **Qualified trainer/evaluator:** The person must have the knowledge, training, and experience to train and evaluate PIT operators. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)

**Required LMS content**
- Truck-specific operating instructions, warnings, limitations, and controls.
- Differences between truck types and attachments used at your site.
- Steering, visibility, stability/center of gravity, capacity/data plates, load handling, stacking, ramps, docks, trailers, battery charging or fuel handling, and pedestrian interaction.
- Operating surfaces, aisle widths, overhead clearances, racking, chemical-spill areas, poor lighting, loading/unloading, and site traffic routes.
- Required pre-use inspection and out-of-service criteria.
- Site-specific chemical-plant rules: hazardous-location suitability where applicable, ignition-source controls, spill reporting, vehicle access limits, loading/unloading operations, and emergency egress.

**Refresher and evaluation**
- Refresher training and an effectiveness evaluation are required when unsafe operation is observed; after an accident or near miss; when an evaluation reveals unsafe operation; when assigned a different truck type; or when workplace conditions change in a way that affects safe operation. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)
- Evaluate each operator’s performance **at least once every three years**. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)
- Certification must include operator name, training date, evaluation date, and identity of the trainer/evaluator. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)

### Bloodborne pathogens

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.1030.

This is not automatically required for all chemical-plant employees. It applies when employees have reasonably anticipated occupational exposure to blood or other potentially infectious materials (OPIM) as part of their job duties—not merely because they could encounter an injured coworker in an emergency. Your exposure determination must identify job classifications and tasks with occupational exposure. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1030)

**Likely covered roles**
- Designated first-aid responders, occupational-health personnel, emergency response teams, security personnel, some cleanup personnel, and laboratory/medical roles—if their duties reasonably anticipate exposure.
- Employees who voluntarily provide incidental first aid may not automatically be occupationally exposed, but your site should evaluate its actual first-aid-response model carefully.

**Minimum LMS content**
- OSHA’s bloodborne-pathogens standard and site Exposure Control Plan.
- Epidemiology and symptoms of bloodborne diseases.
- Modes of transmission.
- Universal precautions.
- Exposure-control methods: engineering controls, work practices, sharps safety, PPE, handwashing, decontamination, regulated-waste handling, laundry where applicable.
- Hepatitis B vaccine availability and benefits.
- Exposure-incident reporting, post-exposure evaluation, follow-up, and confidentiality.
- An opportunity for interactive questions and answers with a knowledgeable trainer.

**Frequency and records**
- Initial training at assignment to tasks where occupational exposure may occur.
- At least **annually thereafter**, within one year of prior training.
- Additional training when task/procedure changes create new occupational exposure; it may be limited to the new exposures.
- Training must be at no cost and during working hours.
- Maintain training records for **three years** from the training date. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1030)

### Chemical hygiene

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.1450, Occupational Exposure to Hazardous Chemicals in Laboratories.

This standard applies to qualifying **laboratory use** of hazardous chemicals—not ordinary production, blending, packaging, warehouse, or bulk-transfer operations. A production QC laboratory may be covered if it meets the laboratory-use definition and laboratory-scale conditions. Do not use a Chemical Hygiene Plan as the sole chemical-safety program for production operations. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1450)

**Program foundation**
- A written Chemical Hygiene Plan must be developed and implemented, must be readily available to employees, and must be reviewed for effectiveness at least annually. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1450)
- Designate a Chemical Hygiene Officer and define the officer’s authority/responsibilities.
- Include SOPs, control measures, PPE/engineering controls, fume-hood procedures, special precautions for particularly hazardous substances, decontamination, exposure monitoring as applicable, and medical-consultation provisions.

**Training timing and content**
- Provide information and training at initial assignment to a hazardous-chemical work area and before assignments involving new exposure situations.
- Inform employees about the standard/appendices, the CHP location and availability, PELs where applicable, signs/symptoms of exposure, and chemical hazard-reference resources including SDSs.
- Train employees on applicable CHP details, control measures, safe handling/storage/disposal, emergency procedures, and the special precautions for substances designated as particularly hazardous.
- The standard leaves refresher frequency to the employer; set a defensible interval based on hazards, task changes, incident history, audit results, and chemical/process changes. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1450)

### Chemical safety

“Chemical safety” is a curriculum category, not one standalone OSHA training standard. In a chemical plant, split it into regulatory and task-based modules rather than creating a generic course that duplicates HazCom.

**Core regulatory baseline**
- HazCom under 1910.1200 for chemical hazard information and employee training.
- Laboratory chemical hygiene under 1910.1450 where laboratory scope applies.
- Process Safety Management under 1910.119 if you have covered highly hazardous chemicals at or above threshold quantities; PSM has separate employee-participation, operating-procedure, training, contractor, PSSR, MOC, and emergency-planning implications.
- HAZWOPER under 1910.120 only if employees perform covered hazardous-waste operations or emergency response to releases at the defined levels—not merely because the plant handles hazardous chemicals.
- Additional substance-specific standards where applicable, such as benzene, formaldehyde, hexavalent chromium, lead, methylene chloride, and others.

**Recommended course architecture**
- Chemical physical hazards: flammables, combustibles, oxidizers, explosives/reactives, corrosives, compressed gases, pyrophorics, water-reactives, peroxide formers, and combustible dust where relevant.
- Health hazards: acute toxicity, sensitization, corrosion, respiratory hazards, carcinogens, reproductive hazards, target-organ effects, dermal absorption, and chronic effects.
- Site-specific process hazards: transfer operations, drum/tote handling, sampling, charging/blending, line breaking, spills, incompatibility controls, waste accumulation, ventilation, bonding/grounding, emergency showers/eyewashes, and evacuation/shelter procedures.
- “Read the SDS” must be translated into actions: selected PPE, incompatibilities, exposure symptoms, engineering controls, spill response limits, first aid, and escalation triggers.

Use the actual SDS set and chemical inventory to generate microlearning per work area and task. The HazCom training requirement includes methods to detect releases, physical and health hazards, and protective measures such as work practices, emergency procedures, and PPE. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200)

### Walking-working surfaces

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910 Subpart D, particularly 1910.22 and 1910.30.

Section 1910.22 requires walking-working surfaces to be clean, orderly, sanitary, free of hazards, capable of supporting intended loads, inspected regularly/as necessary, and corrected or guarded before use when hazards cannot be immediately repaired. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22)

**Training applicability**
- Train workers who use personal fall-protection systems or are otherwise specifically covered by 1910.30 before they are exposed to fall hazards.
- Train workers who use covered equipment, including dockboards, designated areas, or rope-descent systems, before use.
- Provide routine walking-surface awareness to all employees, but distinguish this corporate/site requirement from the more specific mandatory fall-protection training provisions.

**Required 1910.30 fall-protection content**
- Nature and recognition of fall hazards.
- Procedures to minimize fall hazards.
- Correct installation, inspection, operation, maintenance, and disassembly of the personal fall-protection system used.
- Correct use, including hookup, anchoring, tie-off, inspection, and storage.
- Care, inspection, storage, and use of equipment before use.
- Training by a qualified person and in a manner employees understand. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.30)

**Retraining**
- Retrain when workplace changes, fall-protection-equipment changes, or observed knowledge/skill inadequacies render earlier training obsolete or inadequate. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.30)

For chemical plants, include slips from product residue, leaks and washdown, grated platforms, stairs, access to tank-top areas, loading racks, temporary hoses/cables, dock edges, unprotected openings, corrosion, and weather-exposed structures.

### Hazard communication

**Regulatory source:** 29 CFR 1910.1200.

HazCom applies to hazardous chemicals known to be present such that employees may be exposed under normal use or foreseeable emergencies. The program must include container labeling/warnings, SDSs, employee information and training, and a written workplace program. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200)

**Timing**
- At initial assignment to a work area with hazardous chemicals.
- Whenever a **new chemical hazard** not previously covered in the employee’s training is introduced.
- OSHA permits category-based training—for example, flammability, corrosivity, sensitization, or carcinogenicity—if it effectively covers the hazards. Specific chemical information must always remain available through labels and SDSs. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200)

**Minimum training content**
- Requirements of the HazCom standard.
- Operations/work areas where hazardous chemicals are present.
- Location and availability of the written program, chemical inventory/list, and SDSs.
- Methods and observations to detect the presence or release of hazardous chemicals: monitoring, alarms, visual appearance, odor where applicable, process indicators, etc.
- Physical and health hazards of chemicals in the work area.
- Protective measures: work practices, emergency procedures, engineering controls, and PPE.
- Label elements, pictograms, signal words, hazard and precautionary statements.
- SDS sections and how to use the SDS to make task decisions.

**Important 2026 change-management item**
OSHA’s current HazCom text identifies a November 20, 2026 deadline for necessary updates to alternative workplace labeling, the written program, and additional employee training for newly identified physical, health, or other covered hazards under the revised requirements. Build this into your formal regulatory-change workflow and validate exact applicability with counsel or a competent regulatory reviewer before deployment. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200)

## LMS Evidence Package

For each learner-course assignment, retain a record containing:

- Employee name/unique ID, job title, department, work area, supervisor, and language.
- Regulatory standard and paragraph, company program/procedure number, procedure revision, and course version.
- Assignment reason: initial assignment, annual interval, new chemical hazard, equipment change, job change, retraining trigger, incident, corrective action, or qualification renewal.
- Training date, duration, delivery method, instructor/SME identity, and learner acknowledgment.
- Knowledge assessment, passing criterion, completed answers where feasible, and remediation outcome.
- Practical-evaluation form and evaluator identity whenever the standard or your program requires demonstrated proficiency.
- Authorization status and expiration/review date where applicable—for example, PIT operator, respirator user, LOTO authorized employee, or electrical qualified person.
- Supporting records maintained outside the LMS when required: fit tests, medical clearances, exposure monitoring, annual LOTO inspections, PIT field evaluations, and bloodborne-pathogens vaccination/post-exposure records.

A 100% LMS quiz score does not establish practical competence for PIT, LOTO, respirator use, or electrical qualification. Design the LMS as the control center that triggers, tracks, and stores the **field validation**, not as a replacement for it.

## Recommended Build Order

1. **HazCom first:** It is universal across chemical manufacturing and becomes the backbone for chemical inventory, SDS, labels, new-hazard triggers, and work-area assignments. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200)

2. **LOTO and forklift qualification:** These have highly prescriptive documentation and competency requirements and are commonly scrutinized after serious injuries. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178)

3. **Respiratory protection:** Connect each assigned respirator course to exposure assessment, medical clearance, fit-test status, make/model, cartridge-selection policy, and annual renewal. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA2254.pdf)

4. **Chemical safety and chemical hygiene:** Separate production/warehouse chemical-risk courses from laboratory CHP training, so neither group receives irrelevant material or misses applicable duties. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1450)

5. **Electrical, walking-working surfaces, heat, and BBP:** Use exposure-based role matrices and trigger-driven retraining rules. For heat and BBP in particular, ensure you can demonstrate why specific workers were included or excluded from the assignment population. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.30)

The key audit question your LMS must answer is: **“Show me how you determined this employee was trained on the specific hazards, equipment, chemicals, procedures, and emergency actions they actually encounter—and show me the proof they were competent where the standard requires it.”**
