Respiratory-protection training compliance under **29 CFR 1910.134** is a full program obligation—not an annual LMS module alone. For employees required to wear respirators, OSHA requires a written, worksite-specific respiratory-protection program; a suitably trained program administrator; hazard-based respirator selection; medical evaluation; fit testing for tight-fitting respirators; effective training; equipment maintenance; and ongoing program evaluation. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

For a chemical manufacturing site, your LMS should document the training component and trigger the related field requirements, but it must **not** treat course completion as authorization to wear a respirator.

## Regulatory framework

### Primary OSHA requirements

| Citation | Requirement | Training implication |
|---|---|---|
| **29 CFR 1910.134(c)(1)** | Establish and implement a written respiratory-protection program with worksite-specific procedures whenever respirators are necessary to protect health or the employer requires their use | Train against the actual written program, respirator models, tasks, contaminants, emergency procedures, cartridge-change schedule, and site rules |
| **1910.134(c)(3)** | Appoint a suitably trained program administrator | The program administrator must be competent to oversee selection, medical clearance, fit testing, training, maintenance, recordkeeping, and program evaluation |
| **1910.134(d)** | Select NIOSH-certified respirators based on the hazards to which employees are exposed | Course content must align with hazard assessment, exposure monitoring, chemical SDSs, oxygen conditions, IDLH potential, and assigned protection factor—not generic PPE language |
| **1910.134(e)** | Provide medical evaluations before fit testing or required use | LMS completion cannot clear an employee for respirator use; medical clearance must be verified first |
| **1910.134(f)** | Fit test tight-fitting facepiece users before initial use, at least annually, and after certain changes | Training records and fit-test records must be linked, but fit testing is separate from training |
| **1910.134(g)** | Establish proper-use procedures | Train on facial hair, user seal checks, emergency use, limitations, and correct use in the actual task environment |
| **1910.134(h)** | Maintain, inspect, clean, store, repair, and discard respirators properly | Include equipment-specific inspection, cleaning, storage, cartridge replacement, and out-of-service criteria |
| **1910.134(k)** | Provide effective training before initial use; repeat at least annually and sooner when necessary | This is the core employee-training requirement |
| **1910.134(l)** | Evaluate program effectiveness regularly, including employee consultation | Use employee feedback, observations, fit-test failures, incidents, exposure data, and audit findings to determine retraining and corrective action |
| **1910.134(m)** | Maintain required records | Retain medical, fit-test, and program records; document training sufficiently to prove completion and competency |

OSHA requires respirators, medical evaluations, fit testing, training, and maintenance at **no cost to the employee** when respirator use is required. [osha](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/training-library_major_requirements.pdf)

### When 1910.134 applies

The standard applies in either of these circumstances:

1. A respirator is **necessary to protect employee health** because engineering and administrative controls do not adequately control airborne exposure, are not feasible, or are being implemented.
2. The employer **requires** respirator use—even when measured exposures are below the OSHA PEL or another exposure limit.

If you require an N95, elastomeric half-mask, full-face APR, PAPR, supplied-air respirator, or SCBA in a defined area or task, that is required use. You cannot reclassify it as voluntary just because exposure monitoring is below the PEL. OSHA has specifically stated that employer-mandated N95 use constitutes required respirator use and triggers a compliant written program. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2009-07-14-0)

### When it does not fully apply

The full program does **not** apply when an employee voluntarily chooses to wear a filtering facepiece—such as a disposable N95—when neither the standard nor the employer requires it.

Even then, the employer must provide **Appendix D**, “Information for Employees Using Respirators When Not Required Under the Standard,” to the employee. Appendix D cautions users to follow manufacturer instructions, use a NIOSH-certified respirator suitable for the contaminant, and never use a respirator in an atmosphere for which it was not designed. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134AppD)

If voluntary use involves a respirator other than a filtering facepiece—for example, an elastomeric half-mask—the employer must also implement enough of a program to ensure that the worker is medically able to use it and that cleaning, storage, and maintenance do not create a health hazard. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

## Training compliance requirements

### Who must receive training

Assign respiratory-protection training to:

- All employees required to wear any respirator.
- Employees who may wear a respirator during reasonably foreseeable emergency situations.
- Employees assigned to escape-only respirators, if applicable.
- Emergency response team members, industrial firefighters, spill-response personnel, rescue personnel, and SCBA/SAR users.
- Supervisors responsible for enforcing respirator-use requirements and recognizing misuse.
- Respirator issuers, maintenance staff, fit-test personnel, and program administrators, with training commensurate with their assigned duties.
- Voluntary users only to the extent required: at minimum, Appendix D; more extensive controls for voluntary elastomeric or other non-filtering-facepiece respirators.

For a chemical site, course assignment must be based on the worker’s **exposure group and assigned task**, not simply their department. For example, a batch operator conducting charging, sampling, line-breaking, or reactor cleanout may need materially different training than a warehouse worker who only wears an N95 voluntarily during housekeeping.

### Timing

Training must be completed **before initial required use** of a respirator. The limited exception in 1910.134(k)(4) applies only when the employer can demonstrate that the new employee received training within the preceding 12 months covering all elements in (k)(1)(i)–(vii) **and** the employee can demonstrate knowledge of those elements; any training deferred under this exception must be provided no later than 12 months after the prior training. In practice, the employer still needs to cover site-specific hazards, equipment, and procedures before assigning the worker to those tasks. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

The employee must also have a medical evaluation before fit testing and before required use. A tight-fitting respirator user must pass a fit test with the same make, model, style, and size that they will wear. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

A defensible authorization workflow verifies all applicable gates before required use. OSHA does not prescribe the following as one universal sequence; the specific timing requirements are that medical evaluation occurs before fit testing or required use, training occurs before required use, and a tight-fitting facepiece is fit tested before initial use.

1. Complete respiratory-hazard assessment and select the respirator.
2. Obtain medical clearance through the PLHCP.
3. Provide training and hands-on practice.
4. Conduct successful fit test for tight-fitting facepieces.
5. Issue the exact authorized respirator and filters/cartridges.
6. Document task-specific authorization.
7. Verify applicable prerequisites before required use, task reassignment, and each relevant training or fit-test renewal; do not infer a universal annual medical-clearance expiry.

### Minimum mandatory training content

Under **1910.134(k)**, the employer must provide comprehensive, understandable training before required use, at least annually, and sooner when retraining is needed. The employer must ensure that each employee can demonstrate knowledge of the subjects in **1910.134(k)(1)(i)–(vii)**:

| Required subject | What your course must teach for chemical operations |
|---|---|
| Why the respirator is necessary | The actual airborne contaminant, exposure route, work task, possible exposure level, and why engineering/administrative controls alone do not eliminate the risk |
| How improper fit, use, or maintenance compromises protection | Facial hair in the seal area, eyewear interference, wrong cartridge, improper assembly, damaged valves, poor storage, contamination, and skipped seal checks |
| Limitations and capabilities | Assigned Protection Factor, air-purifying versus atmosphere-supplying limits, cartridge breakthrough limitations, oxygen-deficient/IDLH prohibitions, and when to leave the area |
| Emergency-use procedures | Emergency escape, respirator failure, loss of air, cartridge breakthrough, chemical odor/taste/irritation, worker distress, spill/release alarms, communication, evacuation, and rescue restrictions |
| Inspection, donning, doffing, use, and seal checks | Inspection of straps, facepiece, valves, gaskets, cartridges, batteries/airflow where applicable; proper donning/doffing; positive/negative-pressure user seal checks or manufacturer-equivalent checks |
| Cleaning, maintenance, and storage | Disassembly; cleaning/disinfection; drying; inspection; replacement; manufacturer-approved parts; storage to prevent dust, moisture, chemical contamination, UV damage, distortion, or crushing |
| Medical signs and symptoms | Breathing difficulty, dizziness, chest pain, claustrophobia/panic, heat stress, skin reaction, fatigue, or other signs that may limit safe respirator use |
| General standard requirements | Employee responsibilities, employer responsibilities, medical evaluation, fit testing, facial-hair restrictions, inspection, defect reporting, program administrator contact, and no-cost provision |

OSHA identifies these same core elements: why the respirator is necessary; limitations and capabilities; emergency use; inspection, donning, removal, use, and seal checks; maintenance/storage; medical limitations; and the general requirements of the standard. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

### Training must be understandable and effective

OSHA’s requirement is not satisfied by assigning a slide deck, recording an LMS completion, or issuing a multiple-choice quiz in a language the worker does not understand.

The training must be:

- **Comprehensive:** It covers all mandatory 1910.134(k) topics plus the worker’s actual equipment and work tasks.
- **Understandable:** Delivered in a language and literacy level the employee can understand.
- **Task-specific:** Connected to actual respirator types, cartridges, hazards, emergency conditions, cleaning stations, storage locations, and written procedures at the facility.
- **Knowledge demonstrated:** The employee can explain the required training subjects and apply them to the assigned work. OSHA does not prescribe a quiz format or passing percentage.
- **Hands-on practice:** Use the actual assigned equipment for donning, doffing, inspection, user seal checks, defect response, and care. A separate practical checklist is a strong site-program control, but 1910.134(k) does not define a standalone universal practical-certificate requirement. Appendix A does require fit-test subjects to be shown respirator positioning and strap adjustment and to perform a user seal check as part of fit-test preparation.
- **Equipment knowledge:** Teach the employee to identify their employer-selected respirator and authorized filter/cartridge combination and follow its objective change schedule; do not imply that the user independently selects respiratory protection.

For your LMS, use a knowledge check as one element. Pair it with instructor-led or supervisor-observed practice when the site program requires or uses practical validation. Record that evidence separately from online knowledge completion and do not present it as an OSHA-prescribed universal checklist.

## Annual and event-driven retraining

### Minimum frequency

Employees required to use respirators must be retrained **at least annually**. OSHA interprets “annually” as no later than the anniversary date of the previous training. For example, training completed February 1 must be repeated by February 1 of the following year. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/1998-12-23)

### Mandatory early retraining triggers

Retraining is required before the annual date when:

- Workplace conditions change in a manner affecting respirator use.
- A new type of respirator is introduced.
- An employee demonstrates inadequate knowledge or use of the respirator.
- Any other situation arises in which prior training appears inadequate to ensure safe use.

OSHA summarizes these triggers as workplace changes, different respirator types, or inadequacy in the employee’s knowledge or use. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

### Practical chemical-plant retraining triggers

Your written program should explicitly treat the following as retraining triggers:

- New raw material, product, intermediate, solvent, catalyst, corrosive, sensitizer, toxic dust, or other airborne contaminant.
- Revised SDS that changes respiratory protection, exposure controls, acute toxicity, sensitization, or emergency-response information.
- New reactor, blender, mill, dryer, packaging line, dust collector, tank farm, sampling point, transfer operation, or ventilation system.
- A change from nuisance-dust filtering facepieces to elastomeric APRs, PAPRs, full-face APRs, supplied-air respirators, or SCBA.
- New facepiece make, model, style, or size. This triggers both retraining as needed and fit testing before use for a tight-fitting facepiece.
- Change in cartridge/canister, filter, change schedule, cleaning/disinfection process, storage location, inspection requirements, or emergency procedure.
- Measured overexposure, control failure, process upset, release, odor breakthrough, taste, eye/throat irritation, or near miss.
- Failure to conduct a user seal check, improper facial-hair compliance, damaged equipment, failure to wear assigned respiratory protection, or observed unsafe doffing.
- Fit-test failure or repeated difficulty obtaining a fit.
- Employee report of breathing difficulty, dizziness, anxiety/claustrophobia, heat intolerance, or other symptoms affecting use.
- Return from an absence or change in job assignment where the employee’s respiratory-protection skills cannot be confidently verified.

## Training is only one element

### Medical evaluation

Before an employee is fit tested or required to use a respirator, the employer must provide a confidential medical evaluation through a physician or other licensed health care professional (**PLHCP**). The evaluation must use OSHA’s mandatory Appendix C questionnaire or a medical examination that obtains the same information. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

Key compliance points:

- The employee completes the questionnaire confidentially during normal work hours or at a reasonable time and place.
- The employee must have an opportunity to discuss the questionnaire or medical-evaluation results with the PLHCP.
- The employer receives only the PLHCP’s written recommendation, including clearance status, limitations, need for follow-up evaluation, and the confirmation that the worker received the recommendation.
- Do **not** store detailed medical questionnaire responses in the LMS or allow supervisors access to them.
- Medical records must be retained and made available under **29 CFR 1910.1020** requirements. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2009-06-02-0)

### Fit testing

Fit testing applies to users of a **tight-fitting facepiece**—including tight-fitting negative-pressure and positive-pressure respirators.

Fit testing is required:

- Before initial use.
- At least annually.
- Whenever a different facepiece make, model, style, or size is used.
- Whenever physical changes could affect fit, including facial scarring, dental changes, cosmetic surgery, or an obvious change in body weight. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

The employee must be fit tested with the **same make, model, style, and size** that will be used in the workplace. A passed fit test on one half-mask does not authorize use of a different half-mask, a different full-facepiece, or a different size. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

Use OSHA Appendix A methods. The employee must be shown correct donning, positioning, strap tension, and acceptable fit before testing, and must perform a user seal check before beginning the test. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134AppA)

**QLFT versus QNFT**

| Test method | Typical use | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Qualitative fit test, QLFT | Negative-pressure air-purifying respirators requiring fit factors of 100 or less | Not appropriate where the required fit factor exceeds 100 |
| Quantitative fit test, QNFT | Full-face APRs, supplied-air respirators, SCBA, and other uses requiring higher fit factors | Uses instrumented measurement and must meet the Appendix A protocol/pass criteria |

As a practical rule, use QNFT for full-facepiece respirators and any respirator assigned where a fit factor greater than 100 is required. Validate the exact testing method against the respirator type, required APF, and OSHA Appendix A before deployment.

### Facial hair and seal integrity

Tight-fitting facepiece respirators cannot be worn when facial hair comes between the sealing surface and the face or interferes with valve function. Other conditions—such as eyewear, temple pieces, protective hoods, head coverings, facial scars, or PPE configurations—cannot interfere with the face-to-facepiece seal. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

Training should show the actual seal area. Do not permit facial hair (including stubble, a beard, goatee, or sideburns) when it comes between the sealing surface and the face or interferes with valve function. Avoid presenting this as a blanket grooming rule for hair that does not affect the seal or valve.

For workers who cannot maintain a face seal because of religious accommodation, medical concerns, facial features, or job constraints, evaluate a **loose-fitting PAPR** or another effective alternative—provided the hazard assessment supports that equipment. Do not assume a loose-fitting PAPR solves every exposure scenario; selection still depends on contaminant concentration, oxygen level, chemical properties, assigned protection factor, and emergency conditions.

### User seal checks

Every time a tight-fitting respirator is put on, the employee must perform a user seal check. A seal check is not a fit test and does not replace annual fit testing. OSHA Appendix A permits the positive- and negative-pressure seal checks in Appendix B-1 or manufacturer-recommended equivalent procedures. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134AppA)

Your LMS should include a practical check-off for:

- Facepiece positioning.
- Strap placement and tension.
- Cartridge/filter connection.
- Exhalation-valve condition.
- Positive-pressure seal check.
- Negative-pressure seal check.
- Actions if a seal cannot be achieved: leave the contaminated area, adjust/re-don, inspect/replace equipment, and report the issue.

### Respirator selection and IDLH restrictions

Training is not compliant if the equipment selected is wrong for the hazard. Your program must select a NIOSH-approved respirator appropriate to the contaminant, concentration, chemical form, oxygen level, task duration, work rate, environmental conditions, and emergency potential. NIOSH notes that employers must identify respiratory hazards and exposure amounts before selecting the device, and that respirator use must occur within a complete respiratory-protection program. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ppe/respirators/selection.html)

For chemical manufacturing, respiratory-hazard assessment should evaluate:

- Dust, fume, mist, vapor, gas, aerosol, or combination exposure.
- Acute toxicity, corrosivity, sensitization, neurotoxicity, target-organ toxicity, carcinogenicity, and reproductive hazards.
- Oxygen-deficient atmospheres.
- Potential IDLH conditions from spills, confined spaces, line breaks, vessel entry, reactive events, toxic gas releases, solvent vapor releases, or inerting systems.
- Exposure-monitoring data, OSHA PELs, NIOSH RELs, ACGIH TLVs, short-term limits, ceiling limits, and emergency concentrations.
- Air-purifying cartridge limitations and service-life/change-schedule requirements.
- Escape versus entry/rescue duties.

Air-purifying respirators must not be used for IDLH atmospheres. OSHA treats oxygen-deficient atmospheres as IDLH, subject to a narrow exception in the standard. IDLH entry has specific atmosphere-supplying equipment, standby, communication, rescue, and authorization requirements. Train ordinary operators to stay out of unknown or suspected IDLH conditions and follow site emergency instructions; do not train them to “grab a cartridge respirator” for an unknown spill or release.

## Equipment and maintenance training

Your program must train employees on the actual equipment they use and maintain it in accordance with the standard and manufacturer instructions. NIOSH emphasizes that respirators must be used, stored, and maintained in accordance with manufacturer instructions. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ppe/respirators/selection.html)

### Routine user inspection

Before each use and during cleaning, users should inspect, as applicable:

- Facepiece for cracks, tears, distortion, sticky surfaces, or chemical degradation.
- Head straps, buckles, harnesses, and clips.
- Inhalation/exhalation valves, valve seats, and valve covers.
- Gaskets, seals, cartridges, filters, and connectors.
- Lens and lens seal on full-facepiece units.
- Battery, blower, airflow alarm, hose, and connections for PAPR systems.
- Cylinder pressure, regulator, harness, alarms, hose, and facepiece for SCBA/SAR systems.
- Evidence of chemical contamination, moisture, dust loading, or improper storage.

### Maintenance and storage training

Teach and document:

- When to clean/disinfect and who is authorized to do it.
- How to remove equipment from service.
- How to label, segregate, and replace defective equipment.
- Only manufacturer-approved components and replacement parts may be used.
- Cartridges/canisters must be changed according to a written, objective change schedule—not when employees merely notice odor or irritation.
- Respirators must be stored to protect them from contamination, damage, dust, sunlight, temperature extremes, moisture, deformation, and chemical exposure.
- Emergency-use respirators require inspection at least monthly and before/after use; inspection records must be maintained in a manner that identifies the device, inspection date, findings, corrective action, and inspector.

For chemical operations, storing respirators in uncontrolled lockers, process areas, on forklifts, in toolboxes, or near corrosive/solvent vapors is usually indefensible unless the equipment is protected from contamination and damage.

## Program evaluation and audit

Under **1910.134(l)**, the employer must conduct regular evaluations of the program’s effectiveness and consult employees to ensure they are using respirators properly. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

A robust recurring program review (an annual review is a defensible employer practice, not a fixed interval specified by 1910.134) can include:

| Audit topic | Evidence to review |
|---|---|
| Hazard assessment | Current chemical inventory, SDS revisions, industrial-hygiene monitoring, task-based risk assessments, process changes, emergency scenarios |
| Respirator selection | NIOSH approval, APF basis, filter/cartridge compatibility, change schedule, IDLH/oxygen-deficiency assessment |
| Medical clearance | Current PLHCP recommendations; restrictions; confidentiality controls; no expired or missing clearances |
| Fit testing | Correct make/model/style/size; annual status; test method; pass result; physical-change triggers |
| Training | Initial and annual completion; language access; content version; scores; practical competency observations; corrective retraining |
| Field use | User seal checks, facial-hair compliance, correct cartridges, equipment condition, storage conditions, supervisor enforcement |
| Maintenance | Inspection logs, cleaning controls, repair/discard records, emergency-device inspections, replacement-part controls |
| Employee feedback | Comfort, fit, breakthrough/odor reports, communication/vision limitations, heat burden, supply availability, training gaps |
| Incidents and corrective actions | Exposures, overexposures, releases, respirator failures, fit-test failures, observed misuse, audit findings |
| Contractor interface | Contractor respirator programs, fit-test/medical documentation where appropriate, site hazards, shared emergency procedures |

A program administrator should document the review, findings, due dates, responsible persons, and closure evidence. The written respiratory-protection program must be updated when workplace conditions affecting respirator use change. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

## Records and retention

### Minimum records

| Record | Regulatory basis | Retention expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Written respiratory-protection program | 1910.134(c) | Maintain current version; retain superseded versions per company retention policy and incident/litigation hold needs |
| Employee training record | 1910.134(k); documentation is necessary to demonstrate compliance | OSHA does not prescribe a specific retention period in 1910.134(k); retain at least through employment plus your corporate/legal retention period, and preferably longer for defensibility |
| Fit-test record | 1910.134(m)(2) | Retain until the next fit test is administered |
| Medical evaluation / PLHCP recommendation | 1910.134(m)(1); 1910.1020 | Retain as employee medical record under 1910.1020; generally duration of employment plus 30 years |
| Emergency-respirator inspection records | 1910.134(h) | Maintain in accordance with the standard and company record policy; retain enough to demonstrate uninterrupted monthly compliance |
| Program evaluation/audit records | 1910.134(l) | OSHA does not specify a fixed retention period; retain at least until the next audit plus corrective-action closure, and longer under corporate policy |
| Exposure-monitoring records | 1910.1020 and applicable substance-specific standards | Generally duration of employment plus 30 years, subject to applicable standard requirements |

OSHA has clarified that fit-test records need only be retained until the next fit test, while medical-evaluation records—including questionnaires and medical determinations—must be retained under **29 CFR 1910.1020**. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2009-06-02-0)

### LMS fields to capture

At minimum, your respiratory LMS record should include:

- Employee name, ID, employer, department, job title, supervisor, work area, and language.
- Course title, regulatory citations, site program revision, course version, delivery format, trainer/SME, and completion date.
- Knowledge assessment score and passing criterion.
- Practical demonstration result: donning, doffing, inspection, user seal check, cleaning/storage, cartridge-selection knowledge, and emergency response.
- Training status: initial, annual, event-driven retraining, post-incident, new equipment, new chemical hazard, or corrective action.
- Assigned respirator type, manufacturer, make/model, style, and size.
- Authorized cartridge/filter combinations and change schedule reference.
- Medical-clearance status and applicable restrictions/follow-up status—not questionnaire or examination details. Do not assume a universal annual medical-clearance expiration; arrange reevaluation when required by the standard or the PLHCP's recommendation.
- Fit-test status, fit-test date, method, pass/fail result, exact facepiece tested, and next due date.
- Required-use versus voluntary-use classification.
- Supervisor verification and restrictions, if any.
- Program administrator review and final authorization status.

## A defensible LMS course structure

### Module 1: Respiratory hazards and program scope

Cover airborne contaminants at the specific plant; routes of entry; acute and chronic effects; applicable exposure limits; engineering controls; why respirators are used; required-use areas; voluntary-use rules; and employee rights/responsibilities.

### Module 2: Assigned respirator selection and limits

Cover the exact respirator assigned, NIOSH approval, filters/cartridges, APF, limitations, prohibited environments, oxygen-deficient and IDLH restrictions, change schedules, breakthrough indicators, and exit criteria.

### Module 3: Donning, doffing, inspection, seal checks

Use equipment-specific instruction and hands-on practice. Record any site-required practical demonstration separately from online course progress. Fit-test preparation has its own mandatory positioning, adjustment, and user-seal-check steps under Appendix A.

### Module 4: Routine use and work practices

Cover facial-hair rules, eyewear and PPE interference, communication, heat burden, work/rest considerations, avoiding contamination during doffing, leaving the area for malfunction, and reporting issues.

### Module 5: Maintenance, cleaning, storage, and replacement

Cover disassembly, cleaning/disinfection, drying, inspection, disposal, storage, cartridge handling, defective-equipment controls, and issuing procedures.

### Module 6: Emergency response and failure actions

Cover alarm response, spill/release actions, symptoms, cartridge breakthrough, loss of airflow, damaged facepiece, emergency escape, rescue limitations, and prohibition on unauthorized entry into unknown/IDLH atmospheres.

### Module 7: Medical signs, training requirements, and retraining

Cover medical signs or symptoms that may limit use; employee and employer responsibilities; training before required use and annually; the event-driven retraining triggers; and whom to contact for a problem. OSHA requires knowledge to be demonstrated for the listed subjects. The employer may use a trainer checklist and hands-on observation as additional site-program evidence.

### Field validation and authorization workflow (administrator-managed)

Keep this outside the learner course. Before required use, verify the hazard-based selection, medical evaluation, training, fit test for the exact tight-fitting facepiece where applicable, correct equipment issuance, site-specific practical checks required by the employer, and task authorization. Track fit-test timing and medical follow-up separately; do not make medical clearance expire annually unless the PLHCP or applicable requirements establish that date.

## Common OSHA citation exposures

- Treating an N95 or elastomeric respirator as “voluntary” when the company requires it.
- Issuing respirators before medical clearance.
- Fit testing a different respirator than the employee actually wears.
- Allowing employees to swap among facepiece makes/models without a fit test.
- Failing to annualize fit testing and training by the anniversary date.
- Allowing facial hair or PPE interference in the sealing area.
- Using odor as the sole cartridge-change indicator.
- Using APRs for unknown, oxygen-deficient, or potential IDLH conditions.
- Providing generic online training with no hands-on instruction or demonstrated user seal check.
- Failing to retrain after a process, chemical, respirator, or procedure change.
- Storing respirators in contaminated process areas or personal lockers without protective packaging.
- Letting employees use expired, damaged, contaminated, or incompatible cartridges.
- Providing no Appendix D to voluntary filtering-facepiece users.
- Assigning supervisors responsibility without training them to identify noncompliance.
- Maintaining incomplete or mismatched records: the LMS says “approved,” but the fit-test record shows another mask model or the PLHCP clearance is missing.

## Audit-ready compliance standard

A worker should not be considered authorized for required respirator use until the employer can demonstrate all of the following:

\[
\text{Authorized respirator user}
=
\text{Hazard-based selection}
+
\text{Written program}
+
\text{Medical clearance}
+
\text{Training}
+
\text{Fit test, if tight-fitting}
+
\text{Issued correct equipment}
+
\text{Field competency}
+
\text{Current records}
\]

That model matches OSHA’s framework: use only an appropriate, NIOSH-approved respirator within a comprehensive written program that includes medical evaluation, fit testing, training, maintenance, recordkeeping, and program evaluation. [osha](http://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)
